# FIRE Planner

Offline, single-file HTML FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) calculators for a couple (or single person). Nothing leaves your device — open the file in a browser and it runs entirely client-side, with no server, account, or internet connection required.

MIT licensed — see [LICENSE](LICENSE).

## Files

- `index.html` — landing page linking to both tools
- `Planner_US.html` — US version
- `Planner_Canada.html` — Canada version (Ontario / BC)
- `shared/` + `scripts/sync-shared.js` — canonical copies of the code (JS, CSS, markup) that is identical in both planners; `npm run sync` writes them into both files and `npm test` fails if they drift. Not needed to run a planner, which stays a single self-contained file
- `CLAUDE.md` — architecture notes and gotchas for Claude Code (or any AI assistant) working in this repo

## Shared capabilities

Both tools run on the same underlying engine and share these features:

- **Guided setup wizard**: an optional modal (3 steps in the US planner, 2 in Canada), launched from the prominent "✨ Get started" button in the topbar, for getting a first projection running — step 1 asks for the basics (single/partnered, ages, income, retirement age, spending). In the US planner, step 2, "Your balances," asks for taxable and cash plus every retirement account by partner (traditional 401(k), traditional IRA, Roth 401(k), Roth IRA) — no ownership split is assumed — with a prominent note to set the Roth basis, since it decides how much Roth money an early retiree can reach penalty-free. The last step, "A few optional questions," groups the features below by everyday category (Healthcare, Income, Taxes & investing, Guardrails, Debt) rather than one flat checklist, so a novice sees a short, organized set of things to skip rather than eight sophisticated concepts in a row: Healthcare (ACA subsidy, long-term care), Taxes & investing (Roth conversion/RRSP meltdown ladder, mega backdoor Roth), Guardrails (Guyton-Klinger). Every wizard field is a live proxy for the real sidebar control, so it never gets out of sync and can be safely skipped entirely. Every field spells out what it does and doesn't cover — income is explicitly gross/pre-tax, each balance field names one account and one partner (Canada's wizard keeps its combined RRSP and TFSA totals), household spending explicitly excludes housing and healthcare (which have their own fields later, already pre-filled with placeholders), and each step-2 checkbox explains what turning it on actually changes, not just where its section lives. A ✕ button in the corner (in addition to Escape or clicking outside the modal) closes the wizard from any step without leaving the page — useful since a phone's or browser's own back button would otherwise navigate away from the tool entirely rather than just closing the dialog
- **First-run banner**: sits above the two-column assumptions/results layout (not inside either side), so it's visible immediately on load and, on mobile, on both the Inputs and Dashboard tabs without switching. It spells out that the loaded numbers are a generic example, not yours, and points at the two ways to fix that (the setup wizard, or importing a previously-exported scenario file); it clears itself the moment either happens, or the moment you edit any sidebar field directly.
- **Collapsible inputs panel (desktop)**: a «/» button at the top of the sidebar collapses it down to a thin rail, handing that width to the dashboard — useful once you've got your numbers in and mainly want to look at the charts. The choice sticks across reloads (its own small saved preference, independent of your actual plan data). Mobile already has its own Inputs/Dashboard tab switcher and isn't affected by this.
- **Category rail for the inputs sidebar**: a row of filter pills ("Scenarios," "Profile & taxes," "Timeline," "Income," "Spending," "Assets," "Housing," "Retirement & healthcare," "Other income," "Advanced") sits above the assumption groups — click/tap one to narrow the sidebar down to just that category instead of scrolling through all of them, or "All" to see everything again like before. Available at every width, including mobile's single-column Inputs tab (previously desktop-only; mobile hid the rail entirely and always showed every group). On mobile, tapping a pill also scrolls the rail back into view if you'd scrolled down a filtered list, since the rail itself isn't sticky there. A search hit or a click on "Review →" for an estimated field always resets the filter back to "All" first, so it can never jump you to a field a filter is hiding.
- **Full-width layout on desktop**: the page no longer caps itself at 1600px and centers with empty margins on either side of a wide monitor — it now fills the full browser width (minus normal edge padding), so a large screen actually gets used instead of leaving space idle on both sides. The dashboard column (metrics tiles, charts, tables) itself still caps at 1400px so individual widgets don't stretch into oversized, hard-to-read shapes on an ultra-wide monitor — extra width beyond that shows as empty space to the right of the dashboard rather than distorting its contents. The expanded/fullscreen chart view is exempt and always uses the full screen.
- **"What do you want to figure out?" menu**: a small, collapsed-by-default "💡 What do you want to figure out?" bubble right under the plan verdict — click it to expand the full menu in place, rather than a big menu competing with the verdict for attention or getting buried at the bottom of a long page. Only appears on the Dashboard tab (it's part of the results). Each of the seven questions is now a bigger, card-style button with a one-line description under its title, not just a short label — retire earlier or later, buy or rent, Roth conversions (RRSP meltdown in the Canada version), pay off the mortgage or invest instead, what happens if you live 10 years longer than planned, what's actually driving the results, what if returns are worse than expected — each pre-wired to an existing comparison rather than a blank slate. The moment the menu is expanded, each of the six comparison-style questions also grows a one-line delta preview right on the button itself (e.g. "Own → Rent: -$257.1K end assets · lasts to age 72"), so you can see the gist of the answer at a glance without clicking in — it updates live as you change assumptions while the menu is open, and always matches the full comparison a click reveals since both read the same underlying numbers. The two "jump to a chart" questions (sensitivity, historical returns) don't get a preview, since there's no two-scenario comparison to summarize. Most run the current plan against 2-4 alternatives and answer with a plain-language sentence (e.g. "Retiring 3 years earlier costs you $340K and moves your depletion age from 95 to 89") plus the numbers underneath; the last two jump straight to the sensitivity ("tornado") chart or switch the main chart to the historical S&P 500 backtest and report its success rate, switching to the Dashboard tab first on mobile if needed. The mortgage question only appears once you actually own (it has nothing to compare against if you rent). The Canada version's CPP/OAS question compares four points rather than a simple early/late toggle, since CPP's deferral credit is smaller than OAS's and a common real strategy is to take CPP around 65 while deferring OAS to 70 — a single bundled lever couldn't show that. Uses whatever numbers are currently loaded, including the generic example data before you've entered your own. Answers scroll into view automatically (switching to the Dashboard tab first on mobile if needed), and a plan that never depletes is labeled "Age N+" like the other comparison tables rather than "Never"
- **Four-tile dashboard headline, with the rest one click away**: the top of the results shows four tiles — investable assets at retirement, a nest egg for today's lifestyle (the "FI number"), a nest egg for your retirement lifestyle (the "FIRE number"), and the age the portfolio lasts to — with each partner's own retirement age in a small colored box at the top right of the results. Net worth at retirement, bond-reserve funding, the estimated early-retirement age, and the historical success rate (from the same 1928–2025 backtest the Plan Status Bar and "Historical (S&P 500)" chart view use, computed once and shared) sit in a collapsed "More results" section right below. The historical/Monte Carlo success-rate boxes tied to the main chart's "Historical (S&P 500)"/"Monte Carlo" views live with the chart itself, not inside "More results"
- **Collapsed "Methodology & assumptions" section**: the detailed explanation of how the model works (contribution rules, tax treatment, healthcare/ACA/IRMAA or CPP/OAS/clawback rules, and the historical-backtest methodology) is tucked into its own collapsed-by-default section below the projection table, rather than always-expanded text between the table and the footer — so it's there when you want to see exactly what's being calculated, without adding length to every page load
- **"What's driving this" shortfall banner**, and its positive counterpart: when a projection depletes before the end of the plan, a banner explains the likely cause and the smallest change that would fix it. It checks structural causes first (LTC or healthcare costs materially increasing the shortfall) before falling back to the two generic levers everyone has — spending and retirement timing — and for those two, it searches for the minimal cut or delay that actually resolves the shortfall rather than testing one arbitrary fixed amount. When the plan is healthy instead, a green "On track" banner says so directly — the age you're planning to retire, how long the money is projected to last, and roughly what's left at the end — rather than leaving the top of the dashboard silent just because there's no problem to flag
- **Couple or single-person mode**, each partner with independent ages, income, retirement date, and a configurable "plan finances through age" (life expectancy, default 100) that sets how many years the projection runs
- **Investment growth**: separate nominal return assumptions for stocks, bonds, and a high-yield savings/cash rate
- **Bond-ladder bucket strategy**: a configurable number of years of expenses held in bonds, ramped up automatically before retirement, drawn down last
- **Housing**, chosen per scenario:
  - **Own**: existing mortgage — original loan amount, remaining balance, rate, loan term (the original term, not however many years you think are left), and an optional extra-principal slider to accelerate payoff. The payment is fixed from the original loan amount and term, exactly like a real mortgage, so if you've already been paying extra, you don't need to know or guess how many years you have left — the model works that out on its own from your current balance
  - **Rent**: monthly rent with its own growth rate
  - **Rent, then buy**: rent until a chosen age, then purchase a home (cash or a new mortgage with a manually-entered loan term, down payment %, closing cost %, and extra-principal slider) — this is a fresh loan starting from the full purchase price, so there's no "years remaining" ambiguity to begin with
  - **Property tax and home insurance** are separate annual figures (each with its own growth rate, independent of general inflation), so they can be sized to the actual home rather than folded into household spending. Both apply once you own — immediately for "Own," or from the purchase year onward for "Rent, then buy" — and both keep applying after the mortgage is paid off, since they're tracked independently of the mortgage schedule
- **Pre-tax health insurance premium**: a per-paycheck amount (with a paycheck-frequency field: weekly/biweekly/semimonthly/monthly) modeling an employer-sponsored premium deducted from each paycheck while working, reducing taxable wages the same way a 401(k)/RRSP contribution does. This only applies during working years; medications, copays, and other after-tax healthcare costs are expected to be folded into household spending instead. The existing "post-retirement healthcare" field is unchanged in mechanics but now represents the insurance premium only (once retired and under 65) for the same reason
- **Toggleable long-term care costs**: an "Model long-term care costs" checkbox hides/disables the LTC fields entirely rather than requiring them to be zeroed out
- **Add-as-you-go additional loans** (up to 10; start with none, "+ Add a loan"): auto, personal, etc., each with its own original principal, remaining balance, rate, loan term, and extra payment — same fixed-payment-from-the-original-loan math as the home mortgage above, so paying extra doesn't require knowing exactly how many years are left
- **Add-as-you-go household spending changes** (up to 10; start with none, "+ Add a spending change"): each has a new annual spending figure, a start and end age (Partner A's age), and a "Grows with inflation" checkbox — uncheck it for the change's own yearly growth rate. While active it replaces the household spending figure; when two overlap the one that started later wins, and after one ends spending reverts. The Spending section of the sidebar also holds additional (recurring) expenses, one-time expenses, other loans and pre-retirement healthcare; additional income lives under Income
- **Additional income, additional expenses, and one-time expenses — all add-as-you-go lists** (up to 10 each; start empty, "+ Add …" button, × to remove with undo). Income streams and recurring expenses have a start/end age (Partner A's age) and a "Grows with income growth / inflation" checkbox — uncheck it to give that item its own yearly growth rate. One-time expenses (a roof, a wedding, a car) hit at one age and can optionally repeat every N years through a final age; one-time tax-free income lump sums (e.g. an inheritance) are also add-as-you-go. Both one-time lists have a "Fixed amount" checkbox: checked, the amount is the exact dollars at that age instead of today's dollars inflated/grown forward. Part-time or consulting income is just an income stream (the old separate Bridge / part-time section was folded into this list). One-time expenses are excluded from guardrail spending-cut triggers and bond-reserve sizing so a single spike doesn't distort the strategy
- **Add-as-you-go children list** (up to 10; start with none, "+ Add a child"; Profile & taxes): each child has a birth year (a future year counts from birth, for planned children) and a "Full-time student after 18" checkbox. A child counts as a dependent through the end of the year they turn 18, or 23 if a full-time student. In the US planner the dependents raise the ACA household size (a bigger household has a higher 400%-of-poverty-line cliff), so the subsidy and the Roth-conversion guard both see the larger household; a single filer (or the pre-marriage Partner A, who claims the children) with a qualifying child files as head of household (its own brackets, $24,150 standard deduction and capital-gain bands), and a surviving spouse with a qualifying child keeps the joint brackets and standard deduction as a qualifying surviving spouse for the two tax years after the year of death (the year of death itself is a joint return); the federal Child Tax Credit applies for each qualifying child aged 16 or under at year end ($2,200 in 2026, indexed; reduced by $50 per $1,000 of income over $200,000, or $400,000 on a joint return). It first offsets your federal income tax, and the part that is left over is refundable up to $1,700 per child at 15% of earned income over $2,500 (earned income means wages; pension, withdrawals and extra income streams do not count, so a retiree gets only the part that offsets tax). A qualifying child who is 17 or older (17 and 18, and full-time students to 23) gets the $500 Credit for Other Dependents instead: nonrefundable, not indexed, and phased out by the same $50 per $1,000. It assumes every child and parent has a Social Security number. Spending is not adjusted for children automatically. The Canada planner keeps the list (for the Canada Child Benefit and provincial credits to come) but does not use it yet.
- **Long-term care costs** per partner, starting at a chosen age
- **Cash reserve target**, maintained in HYSA; excess flows to brokerage
- **Withdrawal waterfall** in retirement: taxable → tax-deferred → tax-free → cash → bond reserve → home equity (last resort)
- **Cost-basis tracking** on the taxable brokerage account: a starting cost-basis %, with all new contributions added at 100% basis and withdrawals taxed pro-rata on the blended basis/gain ratio
- **Real vs. nominal dollar toggle** for every result
- **Light/dark mode toggle**: defaults to your OS/browser color-scheme preference and can be flipped from the topbar. Deliberately not persisted across reloads (unlike your plan data, below) — it just re-reads your system preference each time the page opens, so it always matches whatever your system is set to right now
- **Nest-egg tiles with an adjustable safe withdrawal rate**: "FI number" (today's household spending ÷ the withdrawal rate) and "FIRE number" (spending in your retirement year ÷ the rate), each with a "% funded" figure measured against your current liquid assets only (taxable + tax-deferred + tax-free + cash — never home equity; today's balances, not a projection). A "Withdrawal rate" box on each tile (default 4%, i.e. 25×; both boxes edit one saved value, and an ⓘ explains what the rate means) re-sizes both. The "More results" section keeps an estimated early-retirement age: the first age, at your current savings rate, your liquid assets would reach the FIRE number if you kept working and saving indefinitely
- **Smoothed/proportional withdrawal toggle**: an alternative to the default "drain taxable first" withdrawal order. When enabled, each year's spending need is drawn from taxable and tax-deferred accounts in proportion to their current balances, rather than exhausting taxable completely before touching tax-deferred — this avoids the sharp jump in taxable income that happens the year taxable savings run out. It applies to every view, including the historical backtest and Monte Carlo, where it replaces only the stock-account part of the withdrawal (taxable and tax-deferred in proportion); in a down year those views still draw cash and the bond reserve first, to protect against sequence-of-returns risk.
- **Cliff plan (US-only, on by default; the Cliff plan checkbox in Withdrawal & tax strategy)**: on the plan's own assumptions (return, inflation, bond yield and the future events your inputs already describe), the planner chooses the years in which to keep income just under a federal cost line within reach of that year's draw (the ACA 400%-of-poverty cliff, the Medicaid line, an IRMAA tier) by capping the traditional draw there, so the rest comes from the order's own next sources (Roth, cash), and the years in which to lift income just over a floor (the premium is lower just over it, for example with Medicaid declined) with penalty-free traditional money, keeping the extra as cash. Each year's choice weighs the premium and IRMAA saved against an estimate of the tax traditional money will owe later. The plan is kept only if it lasts at least as long as no steering and leaves more after tax at the end (traditional money counted net of the income tax it carries); it is also tried with only its ACA years or only its IRMAA years. It is chosen on the plan's own projection, re-chosen only when the inputs change, and applied unchanged in every other run, the retirement-age comparison lines, the sensitivity views, historical backtests and Monte Carlo paths, which follow it by age and never re-plan for their own markets (a year whose line is out of reach on a path, or that cannot keep to the plan, runs as with no steering). The year table's status tooltip shows each year the plan was carried out. The bond reserve keeps its place in the order, penalized money is never drawn to reach a line, and a year with no plan runs exactly as before. IRMAA is priced as the surcharge charged two years later, undiscounted. State-law cliffs and the senior-deduction and child-tax-credit phase-outs are priced by the tax but are not lines the plan aims for. Turn it off to see the withdrawal order with no line steering. Old saves open with it on
- **Optimized withdrawal order toggle (live, US-only)**: goes further than the smoothed toggle above — each year, draws from whichever of taxable or traditional is actually cheaper this year first, fully covering the need from the other if the first runs short (never stranding one bucket to drain Roth/cash/bonds instead), and leans toward whichever realizes less income when that year's ACA-subsidy/IRMAA safe room is tight (the same ceiling the Roth conversion ladder's own sizing already respects). It's a fast, one-year-lookahead heuristic that runs inline in the live projection — same "always current, instantly reflected" behavior as every other toggle — not the deeper, multi-year lifetime search the "Withdrawal & conversion optimizer" card below performs (which can also choose proportional taxable/traditional/Roth splits, not just an order, because it evaluates each choice's actual multi-year consequence directly); for that fuller search, run the optimizer and click its "Apply this schedule to my plan" button. Deliberately never touches Roth itself — Roth is still only reached once taxable and traditional together run short, exactly as the plain default already does. Has no effect on the smoothed-withdrawals toggle above when both are checked (this one wins); same ±1yr/±3yr/CAGR-only scope as smoothed withdrawals
- **Chart x-axis picker**: an "X-axis:" toggle above every chart view (Projection, Historical, and Monte Carlo alike) lets you switch what the horizontal axis is labeled in — **Partner A age** (the default), **Partner B age**, or **calendar year** (starting from the current year) — since reading a chart in terms of whichever partner isn't on the x-axis, or in terms of "years from now" instead of an age, can be hard to do in your head. All three are just different labelings of the same underlying data — nothing about the simulation or which lines are drawn changes, only how the axis and its tick marks are read. The hover tooltip (on the ±1yr/±3yr/CAGR views) always shows the calendar year and both partners' ages together in its header regardless of which one is currently on the x-axis, so you never have to convert by hand. Hidden in single-person mode (no second partner's age to offer), and not part of saved scenario state — resets to Partner A age on reload
- **Five chart views**: retirement date ±1 year, ±3 years, a 0–7% real CAGR sweep, a full historical backtest using actual 1928–2025 S&P 500 and 10-year Treasury bond returns (Damodaran/NYU Stern dataset) and December-to-December US CPI inflation, and a Monte Carlo simulation. The historical backtest only replays starting years that have enough remaining data to cover the full projection length, so a longer "plan finances through age" setting (or a younger starting age) shrinks the pool of usable starting years, while a shorter one grows it. The CAGR sweep's "current" line is your actual entered assumption to the tenths place (e.g. "4.4% (current)"), not snapped to the nearest whole percent, and whichever whole-percent comparison point sits closest to it is dropped so two near-duplicate lines don't sit right next to each other. The legend/line list is ordered top-to-bottom from the highest return (7%) down to the lowest (0%), and its color scale (red = lowest return, teal = highest) tracks each line's real-return rank rather than its position in that list, so the colors stay in the same red-to-teal order regardless of display order. Switching the dollar-display toggle to "Future dollars" relabels each line's percentage as its nominal growth rate (the real rate compounded with your inflation assumption) to match the future-dollar values being charted — only the label changes; the underlying simulated lines are always driven by the real rate
- **Monte Carlo simulation ("Monte Carlo" chart view)**: runs your choice of 500, 1,000, or 2,000 simulated futures and plots the result as a percentile fan chart (10th/25th/50th/75th/90th), alongside a success-rate metric. Instead of drawing each year's return independently at random (which can produce unrealistic strings of 100 years of straight gains or losses), each simulated path is built from randomly-ordered **5-year blocks of real historical data**, so multi-year trends, mean reversion, and the real correlation between stock returns, inflation, and bond returns are preserved within each block. Each block is drawn from anywhere across the full 1928–2025 dataset regardless of how long the projection runs, so — unlike the historical backtest below — it is not affected by the "plan finances through age" setting. Because it draws from the same 1928–2025 dataset as the historical backtest but recombines it into many more independent paths, its success rate is a different (and complementary) number from the historical backtest's — the historical view retells a small number of overlapping real 20th/21st-century sequences, while Monte Carlo dilutes any single bad era across thousands of randomly recombined paths
- **Hover-to-inspect charts**: mousing over the ±1yr/±3yr/CAGR views shows a crosshair and each series' value at that age (disabled on the Monte Carlo view, which shows percentile bands rather than individual paths). On the ±1yr/±3yr comparison (both "Both together" and "Fix one partner" modes), the legend and the hover tooltip list the "farther"/"+N yrs" line first, "Selected" in the middle, and the "closer"/"−N yrs" line last, top to bottom — the same convention as the CAGR sweep, where the highest comparison value is always listed first regardless of which line is actually higher on the chart at a given age
- **Historical backtest: identify which starting year is which**: the historical view draws one line per replayed starting year (red if that year's plan ran out of money, teal if it survived). Hovering the chart highlights whichever line is vertically closest to the cursor — rather than trying to show all 30-70+ lines' values at once, or listing every failing year in the note text below (which could run long on a rougher scenario) — and its tooltip names that line's starting year, whether it survived or depleted (and at what age, if it depleted), and its value at the hovered age, so any individual red or teal line can be identified directly instead of only counted
- **Two-level chart controls**: rather than one row of five view buttons plus a path-count row that only sometimes applies, chart controls split into a primary "View" toggle — Projection / Historical / Monte Carlo — and a second row of options specific to whichever view is selected: ±1 yr / ±3 yrs / CAGR under Projection, or the 500 / 1,000 / 2,000-path picker under Monte Carlo. Switching back to "Projection" from Historical or Monte Carlo restores whichever of the three projection sub-views you were last on, rather than resetting to a default
- **Expand the chart to fill the screen**: a ⤢ button next to the chart legend (bigger and easier to tap on a phone) switches to a full-viewport view — the chart itself gets the space, with the success-rate metric cards hidden and the view/shift/path toggle rows shrunk down and reflowed to sit side by side, wrapping only once they run out of horizontal room, rather than each one claiming its own full line regardless of how little width it actually needs. An × in the top-right corner (or the Escape key) returns to normal. Background scrolling is locked while expanded. On a phone still in portrait, a "Rotate your phone for a better view" hint appears since the chart reads much better in landscape; where the browser supports it (mainly Android Chrome — iOS Safari doesn't), expanding also opportunistically requests real fullscreen with a locked landscape orientation, hiding the address bar and auto-rotating the view, but falls back cleanly to the plain CSS overlay everywhere it can't
- **Hide the chart's option rows to make more room**: a ⌃/⌄ button next to the expand button collapses the whole view/shift/path toggle area — and the X-axis row below the chart — out of the way, handy once you've picked the settings you want and just want to look at the chart itself. It shows up wherever collapsing it actually helps (the expanded full-screen view, where the chart grows to fill whatever space it frees up — this is the main win on a phone in landscape, where the X-axis row otherwise eats a chunk of the bottom of the screen; and on a narrow/mobile screen even without expanding, so there's less to scroll past to reach the chart) and stays out of the way otherwise, since on an ordinary desktop view the chart's height doesn't depend on how much is showing above/below it
- **X-axis picker sits under the chart, and the toolbar rows pack onto shared lines on desktop**: the "X-axis: Partner A age / Partner B age / Year" picker lives just below the chart itself (between the canvas and the "Values shown in..." note) rather than above it in the options toolbar, in smaller type than the other toggle rows since it's reached far less often. On a wide-enough screen (desktop, above the same ~820px breakpoint used for the mobile layout switch) — and always while expanded — the remaining toolbar rows (Projection/Historical/Monte Carlo, ±1yr/±3yrs/CAGR, Both together/Fix one partner plus its Partner A/Partner B picker) sit on the same line(s) instead of each claiming a full line of its own, wrapping onto a second line only once they run out of room, so more of the card's height goes to the chart. On mobile widths the rows still stack, same as before. The "Log scale" checkbox is right-justified against the far edge of this row group (last in the toolbar) rather than sitting sandwiched between two toggle rows, at every width and in both the normal and expanded views
- **"Both together" / "Fix one partner" shift mode** for the ±1yr/±3yr comparison chart (couple mode only): by default, both partners' retirement dates shift by the same amount together, preserving the gap between them ("Both −N"/"Both +N" lines) — the original behavior. Switching to "Fix one partner" instead moves only *one* partner's date, toward or away from the other partner's (fixed) date — labeled by name and resulting age, e.g. "Partner A → 48 (closer)" / "Partner A → 46 (farther)" — which is the more useful comparison when the two retirement dates aren't the same year, since shifting one date alone is otherwise invisible next to a synchronized "Both" shift dominated by whichever partner retires later. Naming the moving partner and their actual resulting age directly in the legend/tooltip (rather than the bare adjectives "Closer"/"Farther" alone) makes clear that only one date is changing, not both moving toward each other — a note under the chart spells this out in words too, including which partner is held fixed and at what age, and when the ±1yr/±3yr window is larger than the gap between the two partners' dates, explains that "Closer" stopped short of the full window because it reached the fixed partner's own date. A "Vary: Partner A / Partner B" picker (shown only while this mode is active) lets you explicitly choose which partner's date moves — it defaults to auto-tracking whichever partner currently retires earlier, but an explicit pick sticks even against the grain (e.g. varying the later-retiring partner's date while the earlier-retiring partner's stays fixed). The whole "Shift:" row (not just the "Fix one partner" button) is hidden entirely in single-person mode, and also whenever both partners are currently set to retire in the same year — reverting to "Both together" first if "Fix one partner" was selected — since with no gap to narrow/widen, "Both together" would be the only real choice anyway, identical to not having the switch at all. The default "Both together" note also points at "Fix one partner" as the way to hold one partner's date fixed, so the alternative is discoverable without switching modes first
- **Plain-language dollar toggle**: the header's dollar-display control reads "2026 dollars ▾" (or "Future dollars ▾"), opening a small panel with "Real dollars" (adjusted for inflation, today's buying power) and "Future dollars" (actual amounts in the year they occur) — the same underlying real-vs-nominal toggle as before, just described in plain terms instead of "Real $ / Nominal $"
- **Quieter top banner**: the top-of-page notice now reads a compact "Planning estimate · Learn about limitations" instead of a full paragraph-length warning. Clicking "Learn about limitations" opens (and scrolls to) a "Limitations & disclaimer" callout at the top of the "Methodology & assumptions" section, which carries the full disclosure — the warning is still one click away, it just doesn't dominate the first thing you see
- **Collapsible sensitivity analysis ("tornado chart")**: shows how far projected end assets swing when one assumption moves at a time, holding everything else fixed, sorted by impact. Five assumptions (investment return, retirement age, household spending, inflation, bond return) are checked by default; five more (HYSA/cash rate, home appreciation, healthcare premium, long-term care cost, Social Security/CPP+OAS) are available via checkbox but off by default. Each swing is a fixed ± amount, e.g. "±2pp" (percentage points) or "±10%", not a probability distribution — a dashed line, labeled with its value, marks the current projection's end assets for comparison. If the current plan is projected to run out of money before the end of the modeled horizon, end assets would show $0 for every single bar (masking real differences between assumptions), so the chart automatically switches to measuring peak portfolio value instead — the dashed line is then labeled "Current (peak)" and a note under the chart explains the switch. Collapsed by default and only computed while expanded, so it never slows down the main dashboard
- **Scenario overlay chart**: once any saved scenario is checked for comparison, a chart appears plotting each checked scenario's total-assets-by-age alongside "Current," so you can see the shape of the difference, not just the summary numbers in the comparison table
- **Guyton-Klinger guardrails (opt-in toggle, off by default)**: a simplified dynamic-spending strategy. - Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)
- **Retirement accounts, per partner** (US): a collapsible "Retirement accounts" group holds four accounts for each partner: traditional 401(k), traditional IRA, Roth 401(k) and Roth IRA (Partner B's are ignored in single-person mode and hidden). The engine keeps one traditional pool and one Roth pool for tax, RMD, ACA/IRMAA and the tables, and underneath it records each account's share of its pool in an account ledger: contributions go to the owner's account (employer match to the traditional 401(k), mega-backdoor money to the Roth 401(k)), and Roth conversions draw from the IRAs first, then the 401(k)s, into the same partner's Roth IRA. Growth, refills and withdrawals hit every account in proportion. Each partner's current share of the pool, which moves with contributions, conversions, discretionary draws and their own RMDs, sizes their RMDs, and each account's own balance sizes its 72(t) payment; each partner's RMD comes out of their own accounts and a 72(t) payment out of its own account (the old ownership slider is gone; enter the real balances instead). A couple whose accounts drift apart (one partner contributes more, or one takes RMDs sooner) therefore sees the RMD split and each account's penalty status follow the real balances instead of a fixed 50/50. When two partners are taxed on separate returns (before a planned marriage, or when the filing status is set to single), each partner's return is charged with the discretionary traditional withdrawals that come out of their own accounts, in proportion to their share of the pool that year, instead of half each. Older saves that used the pooled traditional/Roth fields open with empty retirement accounts
- 10% early-withdrawal penalty: decided per account and per year, not as a blend. Each of the four traditional accounts (IRA and 401(k), per partner) is penalty-free if its owner is 59½ or older or if it is a 401(k) covered by Rule of 55, and penalized otherwise; a discretionary draw takes the penalty-free accounts first and the penalized ones only for what remains (RMDs and 72(t) SEPP payments are never penalized). A deceased partner's accounts pass to the survivor penalty-free. The live withdrawal mix plans over taxable and the penalty-free accounts, and penalized money is drawn afterwards. While any account would owe the penalty, the withdrawal order is: taxable and penalty-free traditional (the withdrawal mix modes apply only here), cash, the taxable bond reserve, penalty-free Roth (contribution basis and seasoned conversions, plus any Roth of an owner 59½ or older), then penalized money cheapest per dollar (income tax plus 10%; a tie goes to traditional so Roth keeps growing tax-free), and home equity last. The historical/Monte Carlo engine follows the same rules inside its cash-and-bond bucket order. Once everyone is 59½ or older or exempt, the original order is unchanged. Pre-retirement bond-ladder and refill moves stay inside an account, so they never owe the penalty
- **Rule of 55** (one opt-in box per partner, off by default): waives the 10% penalty on withdrawals from that partner's own 401(k), traditional or Roth, from the year they retire through 59½; the box is enabled only for a retirement age from 55 to 59 (before 55 it doesn't apply, from 60 there is no penalty to avoid), it never covers an IRA, and a note under that partner's 401(k) 72(t) box says when Rule of 55 already covers it. An old save with the former household box opens with both partners ticked (same numbers) (the planner assumes the 401(k) balance is the plan of the employer you left, not rolled into an IRA); Roth 401(k) earnings stay taxable
- **72(t) SEPP payments** (elected per account: each partner's 401(k) and IRA has its own checkbox and first-payment age, all off by default): models a mandatory, penalty-free annual distribution using the IRS "RMD method" (that account's balance at the start of the year ÷ IRS Single Life Table divisor for the owner's age that year, recalculated each year), from the first-payment age (the owner's retirement age when left at 0, never earlier) through the later of 5 years or age 59½ — taxed as ordinary income exactly like an RMD. A start at 59½ or later has no penalty to avoid, so the election does nothing. While a plan runs, the account is a hard cap: it supplies exactly its payment, nothing more is drawn from it, it is never converted to Roth, and any further need comes from other accounts (taking a different amount is a "modification" that makes the IRS recapture the 10% on every earlier payment; the planner does not model recapture, so the account just gives its payment). A deceased partner's plan ends and the survivor inherits the account. Older saves' household-wide 72(t) flags are dropped, not migrated
- Roth conversion ladder: solves for the annual conversion that fills a chosen federal tax bracket, automatically capped so it never pushes MAGI over the ACA subsidy cliff (400% FPL, when ACA premium tax credits are enabled and not using the enhanced-subsidy option) or into the next Medicare IRMAA tier (once within 2 years of 65, using the 2-year MAGI lookback) — the bracket target becomes a ceiling on the conversion, not a guarantee that the full bracket gets filled. This is US-only, since Canada has no equivalent income-tested healthcare cliff to model (see the RRSP meltdown ladder below for Canada's closest analog)
- **Bracket optimizer** (collapsible, only shown and computed while the ladder is enabled/expanded): re-runs the plan at every available bracket-fill target and reports which one minimizes estimated lifetime tax (income + payroll, summed across the projection in today's dollars), guarding against a recommendation that would shorten how long the portfolio lasts
- **Per-year optimizer** (button inside the same collapsible card, run on demand since it's more expensive than the bracket optimizer above): rather than one flat bracket target for the whole ladder window, independently picks each year's own bracket-fill target — useful because the best target can legitimately differ year to year as MAGI headroom under the ACA cliff/IRMAA tiers shifts, and as the traditional balance itself shrinks. Uses coordinate ascent from three different starting schedules (flat, all-zero, max-bracket) to avoid settling on a merely locally-best schedule, and minimizes estimated lifetime tax **and** healthcare cost together (including any IRMAA surcharge and net ACA premium), with the same never-shortens-the-plan guardrail as the bracket optimizer. Capped at a 30-year window and a fixed evaluation budget so it can't run away on an unusually wide ladder; a year whose recommended conversion comes out to $0 (balance already exhausted by other years' conversions) shows as "—" rather than a misleading bracket label. US-only, for the same ACA/IRMAA reason as the ladder itself
- **Withdrawal & conversion optimizer** (separate collapsible card, always available — not gated on the Roth ladder being on): a wider version of the per-year optimizer above, with three independent levers you can turn on individually or together, each off by default so it never changes your plan until you ask: the per-year **Roth conversion ladder** (same as above); an **optimized withdrawal mix**, which picks each year's own taxable/traditional/Roth split (from a menu including balance-proportional, taxable-only, traditional-only, Roth-only, even-thirds, and a couple of fixed blends) to minimize that year's tax + ACA/IRMAA impact — Roth is a real lever here, not just a last-resort fallback once taxable and traditional run dry; and **smoothed withdrawals**, the simpler household-wide taxable/traditional-only proportional rule, tried both on and off and kept alongside the mix lever as a fast baseline (the mix lever's own "smoothed" candidate reproduces it exactly, so mix-only search is never worse than turning smoothed withdrawals on alone). The mix lever has no separate age window to set — a retiree funds spending every year of retirement, not just during a conversion ladder, so it automatically spans retirement through life expectancy (capped, like the Roth ladder, at 30 years per run). Scored the same way as the per-year Roth optimizer (lifetime tax + healthcare, never depleting the portfolio earlier than the current plan). Like the Roth ladder, this models what a retiree (or their advisor) would actually review once a year with real numbers in hand — this year's income already known, this year's actual ACA/IRMAA thresholds — not a 30-year schedule fixed in stone today. Running the search alone never changes anything — its result table is just a report. An **"Apply this schedule to my plan"** button on that table writes the found schedule(s) into the actual plan (turning the Roth ladder on if that lever was searched, and/or installing the per-year mix as the live withdrawal order for its years) so the main projection — the Selected Retirement Timeline table, every chart, the cash-flow diagram — reflects it from then on, not just this card's own table; it persists like any other input (autosaved, carried in exported scenario files) until cleared with the "clear" link next to the "Applied from the optimizer" note shown above the levers whenever a schedule is active. Applying only touches whichever lever(s) that run actually searched — a previously-applied Roth schedule survives a later mix-only run, and vice versa. US-only. (An earlier **capital-gain harvesting** lever — selling and immediately buying back a slice of the taxable brokerage account to reset its cost basis at 0% tax — was removed after testing found its effect was consistently negligible or zero: any real withdrawal already realizes its own gain at whatever LTCG bracket applies that year, so harvesting rarely found room to add value on top of that.)
- MFJ vs. single filing status, with an optional "marry later" age

## Versioning

These files use **stable filenames** — always `Planner_US.html` / `Planner_Canada.html`, never version-numbered suffixes. Version/date info can live in the on-page header if useful, but the filename itself never changes. This means:

- `index.html`'s links never go stale.
- Every update is just a `git commit`, not a rename-and-fix-links exercise.
- Full history (what changed, when, why) lives in `git log` instead of a pile of old files.

## Testing

`npm test` also checks that the shared regions are identical in both planners, the snapshot of every projection scenario, field wiring and legacy saved-state loading.

`npm test` runs `tests/golden.js`, which checks the core drawdown and compounding math against hand-verified year-by-year figures to the cent. A GitHub Actions workflow (`.github/workflows/test.yml`) runs this on every push. There is no automated coverage of the UI itself beyond this; changes to interactive behavior are currently verified ad hoc (e.g. with Playwright) rather than via a committed test file.

## Making an update

Code shared by both planners lives between `@@shared:<name> begin/end` marker comments; edit the fragment in `shared/` (or edit one planner and run `npm run sync -- --pull <file>`), then `npm run sync`. See `CLAUDE.md`.


1. Edit the file (or have Claude edit it).
2. Save it back over the same filename (`Planner_US.html` / `Planner_Canada.html`) — don't create a new versioned copy.
3. If the change adds or changes a user-facing feature, update this README's feature list in the same batch of work. If it changes anything architectural (a new shared helper, a new gotcha, a new divergence between the US and Canada files), update `CLAUDE.md` too.
4. Commit the change with a short message describing what changed.

Keeping README.md and CLAUDE.md current alongside the code (rather than as an occasional separate pass) is what lets a future session — one with no memory of this conversation, possibly running in a different, disconnected copy of this repo — pick up accurately from just the repo contents.

If you're using GitHub Desktop: open the app, you'll see the changed file listed, write a one-line summary in the box at bottom left, click **Commit to main**, then click **Push origin** to sync it to GitHub.

## Tax-model refinements (both planners unless noted)

- Tax brackets, deductions and credits follow each historical path's own inflation in the backtest and Monte Carlo, not a steady 2.5%
- Dividends and interest on the taxable account and cash are taxed annually (US: ordinary vs. qualified split, counted in MAGI; Canada: eligible-dividend gross-up and credit, counted in net income for OAS clawback), and reinvested dividends raise cost basis. New inputs: dividend yield, qualified/eligible share
- Healthcare and long-term-care costs can grow faster than CPI (`Medical inflation above CPI`, default 1%)
- Fixed: US federal ordinary brackets above the 10% bracket were charged on the whole taxable amount instead of the slice in that bracket (overtaxed in every projection); long-term gain tax now uses unused standard deduction
- Historical backtest and Monte Carlo now tax interest on the taxable bond reserve at that year's actual 10-year Treasury yield (start of year, from Shiller's long-rate series), instead of your Bond return assumption.
- Historical S&P 500 and 10-year Treasury returns now come straight from Damodaran's January 2026 "Historical Returns on Stocks, Bonds and Bills" table (https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pc/datasets/histretSP.xls); the previous S&P series ran about 0.24 points a year higher. Historical and Monte Carlo success rates are slightly lower as a result
- RMD start age now follows birth year (estimated from today's age): 75 for people born in 1960 or later, 73 for 1951-1959, with the Uniform Lifetime divisors for 73 and 74 added
- The historical success rate now says how many starting windows overlap and roughly how many are independent, and the investment return input is labeled as net of fund expense ratios and advisory fees

## Historical backtest and Monte Carlo now follow your plan (Batch 1)

The backtest and Monte Carlo views run the same engine as the main projection, so they honor the strategy you configured. Added one step at a time:

- US: an applied Roth conversion schedule (the optimizer's "Apply this schedule", keyed by Partner A's age) is now used by the historical backtest and Monte Carlo; before, they always used the flat bracket setting.
- Both planners: the pre-retirement bond ladder is now built in the historical backtest and Monte Carlo too (ramped up over the years before retirement and sized once from your plan's inflation assumption), instead of only refilling the bond reserve after the fact.
- Both planners: smooth withdrawals now apply in the historical backtest and Monte Carlo (they used to be ignored there). The same code as the main projection splits the stock-account part of each year's withdrawal; cash and the bond reserve keep their place in the bucket order (first in a down year, after the stock accounts in an up year).
- US: the optimized live withdrawal order (respecting the ACA/IRMAA safe-MAGI room) and an applied mix schedule from the optimizer are now also used by the historical backtest and Monte Carlo, the same way.
- Both planners: the bond reserve is now refilled gradually in the historical backtest and Monte Carlo, instead of all at once. Surplus income refills it as far as the surplus and your bond-years target allow. In an up year, stocks are also moved into bonds, at most one year of that year's expenses (net of one-time expenses) per year: traditional/RRSP first, then Roth/TFSA (these moves create no income and owe no early-withdrawal penalty), and taxable only when neither has anything left (a real sale: the capital gain counts in that year's tax and MAGI, and the tax is paid from the proceeds). Cash and home equity are never used for the refill.
- Both planners (main projection and backtest): the pre-retirement bond ladder is now funded from traditional/RRSP first, then Roth/TFSA, and from taxable brokerage only as a last resort (it used to move half from taxable and half from traditional). Moves inside a retirement account create no income and owe no early-withdrawal penalty; selling taxable stock realizes a capital gain that counts in that year's tax and MAGI, with the tax paid from the proceeds. Projections with a bond reserve change as a result, mostly because a reserve held in a retirement account no longer pays tax on its interest every year; for early retirees, drawing that reserve before 59.5 can owe the 10% penalty.
