How the model works. In couple mode, both partners contribute to RRSP and TFSA while employed, subject to earned income; in single-person mode, only Partner A's limits and income are used. RRSP contribution room is approximated as 18% of current-year wages up to the 2026 dollar maximum ($33,810); unused room is not tracked or carried forward, a simplification versus real CRA room accumulation. TFSA room is a flat $7,000/year per person (2026), also not tracked cumulatively. Employer RRSP matches are calculated as the selected percentage of each working person's salary and added to the RRSP without reducing take-home pay. Optional bridge/part-time/consulting income (per partner, with its own age window) is taxed as ordinary income with CPP contributions but doesn't add extra RRSP/TFSA room. Household spending can include up to five sequential changes, each triggered once Partner A reaches a chosen age and fully replacing the prior spending figure from that point on. Up to three additional recurring income sources (each with its own start/end age) are taxed as Partner A's ordinary income and added to cash flow, in today's dollars; up to three one-time lump sums (e.g. an inheritance) are added directly to cash, tax-free, in the year received. Extra mortgage principal accelerates payoff and reduces cash available for brokerage. A "Rent, then buy" housing mode pays rent (with its own growth rate) until a chosen age, then switches to homeownership: the home's future purchase price grows from today's entered value at the home-appreciation rate, and the purchase is funded first from any FHSA balance, then by cash (down payment plus closing costs, deducted from investable assets in the purchase year) either outright or via a new 15- or 30-year mortgage with its own optional extra-principal payment; from that point on it behaves like Own, including home appreciation, maintenance, mortgage payoff, and home equity in net worth. In this mode, each partner can also contribute a percentage of wages to an FHSA (First Home Savings Account) up until the purchase year, subject to the real $8,000/year and (non-indexed) $40,000 lifetime per-person caps; contributions are deducted from taxable income like an RRSP contribution, the balance grows tax-free, and at the purchase year it's applied tax-free toward the down payment and closing costs — any leftover rolls tax-free into the TFSA balance (a simplification of the CRA rule that also allows a tax-free RRSP rollover instead). The emergency reserve stays in HYSA and remaining excess cash flow goes to taxable investments. The bond reserve is built one projected expense-year at a time and split evenly between taxable and RRSP accounts. An optional RRSP meltdown ladder withdraws from RRSP each year within a chosen age window, solving for the amount that fills Partner A's taxable income up to the top of a selected federal bracket (after any required RRIF minimum), and moves the net proceeds to TFSA (sufficient TFSA room is assumed). CPP, OAS, and pension income offset expenses first; withdrawals then use taxable, RRSP, TFSA, cash, the protected bond reserve, and finally home equity.
Tax treatment. Canada requires each spouse to file an individual return, so this model always computes federal + provincial tax per person rather than jointly; pooled taxable and RRSP withdrawals are split 50/50 between partners for tax purposes unless pension splitting reallocates income between them. Capital gains use the standard 50% inclusion rate — half of each modeled brokerage gain is added to ordinary taxable income and taxed at that person's marginal rate, with no separate preferential bracket. CPP and OAS are fully taxable ordinary income for whoever receives them (OAS is also subject to the separate clawback described below). This is still a planning estimate: provincial credits beyond the basic personal amount, dividend tax credits, the high-income federal BPA phase-out, and CPP/QPP-specific rules outside Ontario/BC are not modeled. RRSP funds are assumed split 50/50 between partners, and RRIF minimum withdrawals begin at each partner's own age 71 using the CRA's prescribed factor table. Brokerage withdrawals use the selected cost-basis ratio to estimate the taxable gain portion.
CPP, OAS clawback & healthcare. CPP and OAS each start whenever selected (CPP 60–70, OAS 65–70) and are added in full to that person's taxable income. OAS is separately subject to the federal recovery tax ("clawback"): 15% of net income (including OAS itself) above the 2026 threshold ($95,323), up to the full OAS amount — modeled using the same year's income as a planning-level approximation rather than CRA's actual one-year lookback. Since Canada has universal provincial healthcare (OHIP in Ontario, MSP in BC), the "extra health/dental insurance" figure only represents supplemental private coverage many retirees carry — there's no premium-subsidy or income-tested surcharge system to model the way the US does with ACA/Medicare. Ontario's own health premium (up to $900/year, income-tested) is folded into the Ontario provincial tax estimate instead, since it's collected through the tax return.
Historical S&P 500 backtest. The "Historical (S&P 500)" chart view replays your exact inputs starting from every calendar year 1928–2025 with a full run of actual data available for your projection length (calendar-year S&P 500 total returns with dividends reinvested, US CPI inflation, and 10-year US Treasury bond total returns, all from the Damodaran/NYU Stern dataset — no equivalent Canadian long-run series is substituted), rather than a single assumed return. It uses a cash-and-bond "bucket" strategy instead of the standard waterfall: the bond pool is sized to your chosen years of expenses; in a year the market fell, that year's spending comes from cash then the bond pool only, so equities are never sold at a loss; in a year the market rose, spending is paid from taxable → RRSP → TFSA and any bond-pool shortfall is refilled the same way. If cash and bonds are both exhausted during a losing stretch, the model falls back to taxable → RRSP → TFSA → home equity to avoid a false failure. HYSA and mortgage rates still use your entered assumptions, since no historical series is loaded for either. Every other feature (taxes, RRIF minimums, OAS clawback, pension splitting, the meltdown ladder, bridge income, spending tiers, the rent-then-buy housing switch) runs exactly as in the standard projection for each historical year replayed. "Success" means the portfolio never fully depletes by the end of your projection in that starting year; it says nothing about calendar years after 2025, which aren't in the data.
